# Duties without maximising

The stance explored in the chat: an ordinary life can be morally permissible while still including substantial positive duties to others. Avoiding wrongdoing is insufficient, but every opportunity to improve someone else's welfare need not become an obligation.

Fairness, commitments, and preventing grave harm at modest cost can constrain personal choices. Within those constraints, friendship, knowledge, health, play, and meaningful projects are goods worth pursuing in their own right.

The objection to maximisation is broader than demandingness: why assume every worthwhile activity belongs in one complete moral ranking? Several ways to spend an evening can be permissible without one being uniquely best. Failure to maximise need not equal wrongdoing; doing more can be admirable without being required.

## A practical spending question

Ask what good the spending actually realises and what competing claim it displaces. A project that gives life its shape has a stronger claim than spending that adds little beyond status or novelty. Price alone does not settle this: an expensive trip may matter deeply, while calling something a passion does not exempt it from scrutiny.

Fair contribution is a starting point for helping. Others' refusal need not transfer unlimited burdens to me, but a serious unmet need at small additional cost can still create a further duty.

**Unresolved:** this gives considerations, not an exact donation percentage or an automatic stopping rule. Pluralism should not become a label that justifies whichever choice I already prefer.
