# Assessing legal judgments

The question is not simply whether I agree with a judgment, but whether it was reached reasonably through a legitimate process.

People can disagree indefinitely about the evidence in a court case. Mere disagreement with the verdict does not justify reopening it; otherwise no decision could ever be final. A serious challenge must therefore identify a procedural or legal failure, genuinely new evidence, or another recognised ground for review.

Justice has to be procedural in this sense. A court's decision is not infallible, but neither can it be treated as defective merely because the judges reached a different view from mine. Judges will differ in experience, temperament, and interpretation, so perfect alignment with my preferences is not a realistic standard.

The useful questions are:

- Was the judgment one a reasonable court could reach on the material before it?
- Was the evidence properly admitted, tested, and represented?
- Were the correct legal standards applied?
- Did the process provide a fair opportunity to challenge the case?
- Is there new evidence or a specific defect capable of changing the legal outcome?

This shifts criticism from substituting my own verdict to examining whether the process was capable of producing a just and reliable decision.
