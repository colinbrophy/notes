# Capability should direct learning

#ai-written

Intellectual development should be measured by what I can now do, not merely by what I can discuss. Information can often be looked up; capability must be trained through practice, failed attempts, feedback and repeated contact with the same class of problems.

Examples include proving difficult statements, building reliable systems, diagnosing subtle failures, analysing messy data and turning vague ideas into finished work. In each case, knowledge has been reorganised into operations and [[Tacit knowledge|judgement]].

## Memory is not a bookshelf

Knowledge does not accumulate cleanly as permanent inventory. Isolated facts fade, details collapse into a vague sense of once having understood them, and much of what passes through the mind leaves little behind. Forgetting is not merely a defect: it keeps every past detail from competing equally for attention.

Repeated use has a different effect. Retrieval and application bind knowledge to situations, making useful patterns easier to reconstruct and eventually turning them into judgement. The important contrast is therefore not stored knowledge versus acquired skill, but temporary exposure versus knowledge repeatedly reorganised through action.

The mind preserves pathways more reliably than inventories. Books and tools can retain details; I need enough concepts, patterns and judgement to re-enter a subject and use them. Do not optimise for how much has passed through my mind. Optimise for what my mind has been trained to do.

## A stopping rule for curiosity

Curiosity alone creates an intellectual random walk: every interesting question exposes another layer, but supplies no reason to stop. A chosen capability constrains what is worth pursuing:

1. Practise the capability.
2. Notice the obstacles.
3. Learn what those obstacles require.
4. Apply it and refine my judgement.

The test for a side question is not only "Is this interesting?" but "Will pursuing it help me do the thing I chose?" Curiosity remains valuable, but needs a budget. An unresolved question is only a candidate for my attention, not a demand upon it.

## Knowing to stop is not being able to stop

A stopping rule is insufficient when I have already judged that an inquiry is no longer worthwhile but continue anyway. An apparently clean, legitimate question can create a loop:

> uncertainty -> discomfort -> one more question -> brief relief -> new uncertainty

Each answer relieves the discomfort while exposing another gap. Continuing remains easy to rationalise because the next question is real and intellectually respectable. It feels like rigour, but rigour requires that the claims I make are justified; it does not require me to resolve every question that appears.

The diagnostic question is:

> Am I pursuing this because the expected answer will change something I do, or because leaving it unresolved feels uncomfortable?

If my judgement already says stop, further reasoning with the next question is part of the loop. The skill required is deliberate disengagement, not better topic selection:

1. Write down the unresolved question.
2. State that answering it is not worth the present cost.
3. Close the chat, book or source.
4. Do not renegotiate when the next legitimate question appears.

The state to practise tolerating is: "There is probably more structure here. I do not currently know it. Learning it is not worth the cost, so I am stopping." Knowing that I should stop is not the same capability as being able to stop.

## One trunk, several branches

Breadth helps with orientation, analogy and choosing where to focus. Without a centre, however, it can become sophisticated consumption. Depth comes from staying with the same objects through definitions, examples, problems, mistakes and correction.

The aim is one deep trunk with several useful branches: breadth should support depth rather than repeatedly displace it. [[SubjectLeverage]] can help choose supporting knowledge, but usefulness is tested through practice.

## Commit without certainty

Searching for the perfect capability is another form of drift. A path only needs to be interesting enough to revisit, meaningful enough to matter, deep enough to sustain improvement and accessible enough to practise now. Commitment itself reveals better problems that are invisible from outside the field.

A practical horizon is one year of exercises, projects, feedback and finished work. The question is not "What was I meant to master?" but "Which good path am I willing to make mine for now?"
